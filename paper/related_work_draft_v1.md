## 2. Related Work

This work sits at the intersection of five research areas: full-duplex spoken language models, retrieval-augmented generation, social robot action planning, voice-gesture output coordination, and safety frameworks for mental health AI. The following sections survey each area, identifying relevant prior work and positioning the proposed system relative to it.

---

### 2.1 Full-Duplex Spoken Language Models

Traditional spoken dialogue systems are constructed as cascaded pipelines in which automatic speech recognition (ASR), a language model, and text-to-speech (TTS) processing operate sequentially. Such pipelines introduce compounding latency, discard non-linguistic information carried in prosody and timing, and require the system to segment user turns before responding — precluding the overlapping speech and real-time backchanneling that characterize natural conversation. Recent work has addressed these limitations by modeling spoken dialogue directly in the audio domain.

Kharitonov et al. [TACL, 2023] (E3) introduced the first textless dual-channel spoken dialogue model, demonstrating that two speakers' audio streams can be modeled in parallel without text supervision. Trained on 2,000 hours of two-channel conversational audio, the model generates naturalistic turn-taking behavior and provides the conceptual basis for simultaneous user-and-system channel processing. The audio tokenization approach underlying later full-duplex systems traces to Borsos et al. [IEEE/ACM TASLP, 2023] (E2), who established a hierarchical two-level audio tokenization combining semantic tokens — derived from a masked language model to capture linguistic content — with acoustic tokens from a neural audio codec. This semantic-before-acoustic principle substantially improves the linguistic coherence of generated speech, and it informs the Inner Monologue mechanism used in subsequent architectures.

Ma et al. [AAAI 2025] (E4) demonstrated that fusing a speaking channel with a streaming self-supervised listening encoder at the token level enables real-time full-duplex interaction, showing that three fusion strategies (early, middle, and late fusion) are viable and that the model can detect turn-taking signals in real time. A concurrent system presented at NeurIPS 2024 [NeurIPS 2024] (E5) — whose full author list is pending confirmation — augments a large language model with a neural finite state machine that emits both response tokens and control tokens governing turn management, achieving response latency below 500ms in more than half of evaluated interactions; this represents a greater-than-threefold reduction compared to a half-duplex LLM baseline under equivalent conditions.

Défossez et al. from Kyutai [arXiv:2410.00037, 2024] (E1) introduced Moshi, described in a recent preprint as a speech-text foundation model for real-time dialogue. As peer-reviewed publication has not been confirmed at the time of writing, Moshi is cited as an architectural reference: the system reports approximately 200ms practical response latency and features an Inner Monologue mechanism that generates text prefix tokens before audio tokens, with separate modeling of the system's own speech and the user's speech in parallel streams. A recent preprint survey [arXiv:2509.14515, 2025] (E7) proposes a taxonomy of full-duplex spoken language models distinguishing Engineered Synchronization (modular, pipeline-based approaches) from Learned Synchronization (end-to-end trained architectures), alongside a four-pillar evaluation framework covering Temporal Dynamics, Behavioral Arbitration, Semantic Coherence, and Acoustic Performance; this taxonomy is cited as a reference frame pending its own peer-reviewed validation.

Across these systems, a consistent limitation is factual grounding: spoken language models trained on speech data exhibit knowledge gaps that scaling alone does not efficiently address, as the training corpora for speech are orders of magnitude smaller than text corpora. This motivates the retrieval-augmented approaches discussed next.

### 2.2 Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Adaptive Retrieval

Lewis et al. [NeurIPS 2020] (E8) defined the canonical RAG formulation as the combination of a parametric sequence-to-sequence generator with a non-parametric dense vector index. At inference time, the top-k retrieved documents condition the generator, allowing factual content to be updated by modifying the index rather than retraining the model. The parametric-plus-non-parametric two-memory structure — and the two retrieval formulations it introduced (per-sequence and per-token) — remain the reference architecture against which subsequent developments are measured. Gao et al. [arXiv:2312.10997, 2023] (E9) survey the progression of RAG into three tiers: Naive RAG (simple retrieve-then-read), Advanced RAG (retrieval optimization at the pre- and post-processing stages), and Modular RAG (decomposition into independently configurable pipeline components). This taxonomy, currently available as a preprint pending peer-reviewed publication, is used here as a reference vocabulary for characterizing the proposed system's integration design.

The principal engineering tension in RAG for interactive systems is latency. Standard RAG retrieves on every generation step, adding the latency of an external index query to each forward pass. Jiang et al. [EMNLP 2023] (E10) addressed this with Forward-Looking Active Retrieval (FLARE), an anticipatory mechanism that generates a prediction of the upcoming sentence and triggers retrieval only when the prediction contains low-confidence tokens; no retrieval is performed when the model is sufficiently confident. This forward-looking trigger — retrieving in anticipation of an information need rather than reacting to a generation failure — is the closest text-domain conceptual precedent for the temporal exploitation strategy used in the proposed system's asynchronous dispatch mechanism. Asai et al. [ICLR 2024, Oral] (E11) extended selective retrieval to its logical end by training reflection tokens directly into the language model, enabling it to decide at inference time whether retrieval is needed, evaluate the relevance and quality of retrieved passages, and revise its output accordingly; Self-RAG improved factual accuracy on open-domain question answering and fact verification tasks over both always-retrieve and never-retrieve baselines.

For spoken dialogue systems, retrieval that operates through ASR introduces an additional processing step that compounds latency. Chen et al. [ACL 2025] (E6) demonstrated, in the first audio-native RAG framework (WavRAG), that processing raw audio directly for retrieval query embedding — bypassing ASR entirely — achieves a tenfold acceleration over ASR-mediated RAG pipelines while maintaining comparable retrieval accuracy, through a text-audio hybrid knowledge base and a chain-of-thought reasoning integration. Arora et al. [arXiv:2510.02044, 2025] (E12) propose, in emerging work under peer review, a streaming parallel query prediction approach for spoken dialogue: the system predicts and dispatches retrieval tool calls in parallel with ongoing user speech, exploiting the temporal gap between turn onset and the delivery of key informational content. On a spoken question-answering benchmark, streaming parallel prediction reduced tool-use latency by 20% and improved accuracy substantially relative to non-streaming retrieval; as this work is under peer review, these figures are reported as preliminary.

The present system builds upon the MoshiRAG paradigm [arXiv:2604.12928, 2026], an architectural reference currently under peer review that proposes asynchronous knowledge retrieval as a module for full-duplex speech language models, exploiting the natural temporal gap between spoken response onset and informational content delivery. **[MATERIAL GAP MG-01: MoshiRAG is a preprint under review; it is treated as an architectural reference, not established prior art.]** The proposed system extends this paradigm to a mental health-specific knowledge base under safety constraints. **[MATERIAL GAP MG-02: Asynchronous RAG as a peer-reviewed architectural category for full-duplex spoken dialogue does not yet exist; the proposed work is among the first peer-reviewed contributions to this sub-field.]**

### 2.3 Social Robot Action Planning and Gesture Generation

Two design philosophies govern robot gesture production. Free-form synthesis approaches train models to produce continuous joint trajectories from speech or text inputs, maximizing expressive range. Predefined command selection approaches maintain a finite vocabulary of verified behaviors, trading expressiveness for safety analyzability and deterministic execution. For systems operating in safety-constrained domains, the choice between these approaches has implications beyond gesture aesthetics.

On the expressive synthesis side, Liu et al. [ECCV 2022] (E19) established the BEAT dataset and a semantic taxonomy of co-speech gestures comprising beat, deictic, iconic, and metaphoric types, annotated with onset-stroke-retraction phase structure across 76 hours of multi-modal recordings. The BEAT taxonomy provides the reference vocabulary adopted in this work for defining the proposed system's gesture command set. The timing data in BEAT is derived from human motion capture of human-to-human conversation and is not directly applicable to robot gesture dispatch timing — a distinction maintained throughout this paper. More recently, Galatolo and Winkle [Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 2025] (E17) demonstrated that simultaneous text and gesture generation can be achieved by attaching lightweight gesture intent heads to the architecture of a small language model, producing platform-specific gesture commands for Pepper (bodily gestures) and Furhat (facial expressions) in parallel with text generation at negligible additional computational and memory overhead. This result establishes that parallel generation of speech and gesture intent is architecturally feasible within the inference budget of deployable robot hardware.

On the predefined command selection side, Colledanchise and Ögren [CRC Press, 2018] (E13) provide the foundational formalization of Behavior Trees (BTs) as a robot control architecture. BTs organize behavior hierarchically through Sequence, Fallback, Parallel, and Decorator nodes, with execution driven by a tick signal that propagates through the tree and returns Success, Failure, or Running statuses. The formal properties of BTs — modularity through subtree reuse, reactivity through tick-driven execution, and failure recovery through Fallback nodes — generalize those of Finite State Machines (FSMs) while supporting formal state-space safety and liveness analysis. Ghzouli et al. [IEEE TSE, 2023] (E14) conducted an empirical study of BT and FSM usage across over 80 open-source ROS applications, finding that BTs offer greater scalability and readability than FSMs as task complexity increases — a finding that directly motivates the BT choice for a system requiring multiple gesture command types, safety mode states, and context-dependent conditional logic. A survey by Iovino et al. [Robotics and Autonomous Systems, 2022] (E15) documents BT applications across more than 160 robotics and AI papers, confirming BTs as the dominant formalism for modular robot behavior selection in social robot contexts. Cooper and Lemaignan [HRI 2022] (E16) deployed a ROS-compatible BT architecture for long-term social robot behavior in a care-support setting (elderly support), demonstrating that BT-based action planning is viable for extended human-robot interaction in a care-adjacent domain; the proposed system draws an analogical relationship to this work while acknowledging that elderly support and mental health support differ in clinical risk profile and should not be equated.

An orthogonal component is the mapping from dialogue context to gesture selection. A peer-reviewed study in Frontiers in Robotics and AI [Frontiers, 2023] (E18) demonstrated large language model-based real-time emotion generation for social robot dialogue, establishing a precedent for LLM-driven behavior selection in the context of HRI; specific architectural details from this work await full-text verification before citation of individual mechanisms. **[MATERIAL GAP MG-03: No peer-reviewed paper addresses asynchronous predefined gesture command dispatch within a continuously operating full-duplex spoken dialogue framework.] [MATERIAL GAP MG-04: No peer-reviewed system combines a predefined gesture vocabulary, full-duplex dialogue, and mental health safety constraints as a unified architecture.]**

### 2.4 Voice-Gesture Output Coordination and Multimodal Synchronization

Coordinating voice and gesture output requires both a principled timing model and an architectural scheduling mechanism. The timing question — when should a gesture onset relative to the lexical content it accompanies — has been studied extensively in human gesture research. The BEAT phase structure [Liu et al., ECCV 2022] (E19) identifies gesture preparation, pre-stroke hold, stroke, post-stroke hold, and retraction as the standard gesture lifecycle. Research on human speech-gesture synchrony has found that gesture stroke onset typically precedes the corresponding lexical affiliate in speech, facilitating listener comprehension. These human timing regularities provide a reference frame; their direct transfer to robot gesture dispatch timing requires explicit engineering validation beyond what the human data alone can provide, as the production mechanisms differ.

In human-robot interaction, the timing of interactive signals matters for user experience. Research on robot backchannels [Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 2023] (P1) has shown that the timing of robot listening signals affects users' subjective perception of robot engagement — a user-perception finding that motivates timing-aware design in the proposed system's output coordination. Paierl et al. [INTERSPEECH 2025] (E21) demonstrated continuous real-time prediction of backchannel timing for HRI, providing a timing prediction precedent applicable to interactive robot signal dispatch. It is important to distinguish this from gesture command dispatch: backchannel timing concerns the emission of listening signals during the user's speaking turn, while gesture command dispatch concerns the emission of gesture commands during the robot's speaking turn. These are distinct timing problems addressed at different conversational moments and by different model components; conflating them would misrepresent both contributions.

Fujii et al. [Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 2022] (E20) built and open-sourced a complete voice-and-gesture dialogue robot pipeline integrating ESPnet speech recognition, a Rasa dialogue manager, and NAO robot actuation through ROS. The dialogue manager outputs utterances with associated gesture commands linked to speech content, and the system has been demonstrated in a deployed setting. This work provides the clearest peer-reviewed precedent for voice-and-gesture output coordination in a real dialogue robot. However, the architecture operates under half-duplex (turn-taking) assumptions — the robot generates speech and gestures within a bounded speaking turn, without concurrent user speech. The proposed system extends this coordination to the full-duplex setting, where voice generation and gesture dispatch must proceed concurrently with ongoing user input and without the natural break of turn-switching. **[MATERIAL GAP MG-05: No peer-reviewed timing model specifies predefined gesture command dispatch parameters under full-duplex voice generation latency constraints; all existing timing models presuppose half-duplex interaction.] [MATERIAL GAP MG-07: The available peer-reviewed evidence for voice-gesture coordination is predominantly half-duplex; full-duplex generalization is a design contribution of the proposed system.]**

Lee et al. [arXiv:2602.04157, 2026] (E22) describe, in a recent preprint, a real-time multimodal language model that interleaves conversational generation with structured action tool calls — gaze and attention direction — for situated HRI, evaluating turn-level tool-decision correctness against human annotation. As a preprint, this work provides an architectural precedent for the concurrent-generation-plus-structured-dispatch pattern at the core of the proposed system; the specific tool set (gaze actions) differs from gesture commands and should not be treated as a precedent for gesture command dispatch.

### 2.5 Safety and Ethics for AI in Mental Health

The deployment of AI in mental health support contexts requires safety and governance designs that go beyond general AI best practices. The core challenge is scope control: a system optimized for conversational fluency may, absent explicit constraints, perform functions — diagnosis, treatment guidance, crisis management — that require clinical expertise and accountability structures not currently possessed by AI systems.

The World Health Organization's 2024 guidance on AI ethics for health, updated specifically to address large multimodal models [WHO, 2024] (S2), identifies accountability gaps, automation bias, and the absence of human oversight as key risks. The guidance establishes six principles — transparency, safety, accountability, equity, privacy, and sustainability — as foundational requirements for any AI system operating in a health context, with human oversight identified as non-negotiable for high-stakes interactions. Addressing the mental health context specifically, a 2024 analysis in JMIR Mental Health [JMIR, 2024] (S3) argues that general responsible-AI frameworks are insufficient for mental health AI because they fail to account for the relational dimension of care: the absence of a defined duty of care toward users and the risk of emotional manipulation by systems that present as empathetic are underaddressed in framework-level guidance. Both sources are normative analyses rather than empirical studies; they establish ethical obligations rather than empirically measuring their effects.

The appropriate system architecture for mental health AI has been characterized through a stepped-care model: a conversational AI system functions as a low-intensity psychoeducational support tier, while presentations of greater clinical severity are escalated to human professionals [Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2026] (S4). This framework, proposed in the perinatal mental health context — with the caveat that generalization to other populations requires further study — informs the functional boundary design of the proposed system. An empirical study in Scientific Reports [Sci. Rep., 2025] (S5) evaluated existing mental health chatbot agents on suicidal ideation detection and management, finding that none met initial criteria for adequate crisis response and fewer than half met minimal criteria. These findings demonstrate the inadequacy of current systems and motivate the explicit crisis escalation requirements adopted in the proposed design; they do not characterize the proposed system's performance, which requires independent evaluation. The necessity of human oversight as an active design element — not a reactive fallback — is established in a 2025 high-impact study [Nat. Med., 2025] (S6), grounding the human-in-the-loop requirements at Tier 3 of the proposed safety architecture.

Data governance constitutes a distinct safety dimension. A 2025 analysis [PMC, 2025] (S7) established that mental health interaction data constitutes sensitive personal data under GDPR Article 9 and protected health information under HIPAA, requiring explicit informed consent, data minimization, and secure storage. These requirements constrain the data architecture of the proposed system; compliance with Japanese national privacy regulations (APPI, MHLW guidelines) requires separate legal analysis outside the scope of this paper [MATERIAL GAP MG-09]. **[MATERIAL GAP MG-08: No peer-reviewed safety framework addresses the additional risk dimensions introduced by voice modality and physical embodiment in mental health robot systems; all existing frameworks address text-based chatbot contexts. Adapting these frameworks to voice-based embodied robots is a design contribution of the proposed system.] [MATERIAL GAP MG-10: No peer-reviewed standard defines safety constraints for gesture command execution in mental health robot interaction; the Tier 4 requirements proposed in this work are derived from engineering first principles and constitute a novel contribution without direct literature precedent.]**

### 2.6 Integration Gap

The five research areas surveyed above have developed largely in parallel. Full-duplex spoken language models have reached interactive latency targets but lack domain-specific factual grounding. Retrieval-augmented generation provides the mechanism for that grounding, and anticipatory retrieval techniques preserve low latency; however, asynchronous RAG as a unified architectural category for full-duplex voice dialogue systems has not received peer-reviewed formalization. Social robot behavior planning via Behavior Trees provides modularity and formal safety analyzability over predefined command vocabularies, and parallel gesture generation has been demonstrated at negligible overhead; nevertheless, no peer-reviewed system has addressed asynchronous predefined gesture command dispatch within a continuously operating full-duplex spoken dialogue framework. Voice-gesture output coordination architectures exist in the literature, but they operate under half-duplex assumptions that do not account for the concurrent-generation demands of full-duplex voice dialogue. Safety and ethics frameworks for mental health AI are well-developed for text-based chatbots but do not address voice-based embodied systems or provide standards for gesture-level safety constraints.

No prior peer-reviewed system integrates all five of these components — full-duplex spoken dialogue, asynchronous knowledge retrieval from a domain-specific base, predefined gesture command planning conditioned on dialogue phase and user emotional state, voice-gesture synchronization under full-duplex latency constraints, and safety constraints appropriate for mental health support interaction — into a single unified architecture. The present paper proposes a system designed to address this integration gap. The scope of the proposed contribution is explicitly engineering-oriented: the system supports psychoeducational interaction and information delivery, not diagnosis, treatment, medication advice, or autonomous crisis intervention. Evaluation of the system's properties is bounded accordingly, separating engineering correctness (RQ1, RQ2) from user perception of the interaction (RQ3) from clinical outcome claims, which are outside scope.
